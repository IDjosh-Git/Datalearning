01

FOUNDATION · 12 MIN

What is data analysis?

Data is recorded information: dates, prices, quantities, survey answers, journey times or electricity readings. Data analysis is the process of examining that information to answer a question, explain what happened and support a decision. The key is to find meaning, not merely to produce a spreadsheet or colourful chart.

One-minute example

You spent ₦2,000, ₦3,000 and ₦1,000 on transport over three days. The records are data. Adding them gives ₦6,000, a summary. Noticing that Tuesday was the most expensive day is an insight. Checking whether a cheaper route works on Tuesdays is a possible action.

Information, insight and decisions are different

RecordWhat happened?

Tuesday transport cost ₦3,000.

ExplainWhat does it mean?

It cost more than the other two days.

Act and measureWhat next?

Try another route; record time and cost.

Analysis can describe the past, explore why something happened, estimate a possible future or compare options. A forecast is uncertain; it is not a promise. You do not need to master statistics or coding before you can begin asking useful questions.

Try it (2 minutes)

Write down one number you record in daily life, a question it could answer, and one decision you might make after checking the answer.

02

RELEVANCE · 15 MIN

Why learn data analysis? You already use it.

Data analysis gives you a repeatable way to check assumptions instead of relying entirely on memory or guesswork. It can help you spend more wisely, ask sharper questions at work, and understand the figures you see in the news. It does not replace judgment, lived experience or professional advice.

💰

Personal money

Track groceries and transport for a month. Separate necessary expenses from optional ones, then compare the actual total with your budget.

Question: Where did my spending change?
🛒

Shopping decisions

Compare the cost per 100 g of two products rather than assuming the larger package is always cheaper. Check quality and how much you will actually use.

Question: Which option meets my needs for less?
🚌

Travel and time

Record journey duration across several days. Compare both the typical time and delays before choosing a route.

Question: Which route is more reliable?
🏪

Small business

Separate sales from profit, identify frequently sold items, and check whether customers buy more on specific days.

Question: What should I restock?
⚡

Household resources

Compare electricity usage or mobile-data consumption over time. Investigate changes before assuming what caused them.

Question: When did usage increase?
📚

Learning and community

Track study hours against topics understood, or compare community survey responses to identify recurring needs.

Question: What needs attention first?
Why employers value these skills

Teams in finance, operations, logistics, education, marketing and health all need people who can define a metric, find reliable evidence, check calculations and explain results clearly. The ability to ask good questions and communicate limitations is useful even when your job title is not “Data Analyst.”

Try it (5 minutes)

Think of a purchase you make regularly. Record its price and quantity on three occasions. Ask whether the unit price, rather than only the package price, has increased.

03

WORKFLOW · 15 MIN

How to turn a question into a decision

Good analysis starts with a specific question. “How is my business doing?” is broad. “What was our net profit each day last week, and which days fell below ₦5,000?” is something you can actually test.

  1. Define the question and audience.

    Who needs the answer? What period matters? What decision will it support? Agree on what words such as sales or profit mean.

  2. Collect appropriate data.

    Gather receipts, spreadsheet entries or database records. Use only information you need and have a right to use.

  3. Inspect and clean.

    Check empty cells, duplicate rows, inconsistent dates, mistaken prices and entries in the wrong units. Record corrections.

  4. Calculate and compare.

    Create relevant totals, averages, rates or time trends. Check the calculation independently before trusting a chart.

  5. Interpret and communicate.

    Explain what the evidence supports, what it does not support, and what could change the conclusion.

  6. Take a measured action.

    Recommend a small test, decide what to measure next, and check whether the action actually helps.

Better questions produce better answers

Weak: “Do customers like my shop?” Better: “Of the 30 customers who answered our anonymous survey this week, how many rated waiting time as satisfactory?” That answer only represents those who responded—not necessarily every customer.

Try it (3 minutes)

Rewrite “My transport is expensive” as a measurable question. Include a cost, a comparison and a period—for example, the average weekly cost for two possible routes over four weeks.

04

DATA LITERACY · 18 MIN

Understand rows, columns and what numbers mean

A dataset is often a table. A row is one observation, such as one sale; a column is one characteristic, such as the sale date or amount. Before analysing, know the grain: what does a single row represent?

Example: one row per purchase, not one row per customer
Purchase IDDateItemAmount (₦)
001MondayRice4,000
002MondayFruit1,500
003TuesdayRice4,200

This table has three purchases, but only two distinct items. If you count rows and call that the number of customers, you would be answering a different question from what the data supports.

Common data types

  • Number: quantity, price, distance (check units).
  • Category: product, payment type, town.
  • Date/time: order date or journey start.
  • Text: feedback, notes or descriptions.
  • Boolean: yes/no, completed/not completed.

Questions to ask first

  • Does one row mean a transaction or a person?
  • Is this figure a price, revenue or profit?
  • Which currency and time period apply?
  • Are totals already included as rows?
  • Does the dataset include every event or just a sample?
Try it (4 minutes)

Using the purchase table: count rows, count distinct items, then sum the three amounts. Answer: 3 purchases, 2 items and ₦9,700 total spending.

05

QUALITY & ETHICS · 17 MIN

Clean, check and protect data

Even correct-looking formulas can produce misleading answers when the underlying records are wrong. Your job is not to make all problems disappear; it is to identify issues, correct what can be verified and describe what remains uncertain.

A tiny data-quality investigation
RecordIssueBetter next step
₦1,500 and 1500Mixed formattingConvert safely to one numeric currency field.
Receipt 028 entered twicePossible duplicateCheck the receipt ID and source before removing a row.
Cost is blankMissing valueAsk for the source; do not automatically treat it as zero.
“Mon”, “Monday”, “monday”Inconsistent categoriesStandardise after confirming they mean the same thing.

Responsible analysis matters

Only collect necessary personal information. Remove names or phone numbers when they are not needed, use access controls, and do not paste someone else’s private information into a public tool or AI chat. Watch for selection bias: a survey of only your happiest customers cannot represent everyone. A pattern between two measures also does not prove one caused the other.

Health, money and safety

Your own records can help you notice patterns and prepare better questions. They are not a medical diagnosis, a financial guarantee or proof of cause and effect. Decisions with serious consequences need suitable expert input and stronger evidence.

Try it (5 minutes)

A spending sheet has two identical receipt IDs but different dates. Would you delete one automatically? No. First check the original receipts. They might be a true duplicate or two legitimate purchases with a bad ID.

06

CORE SKILLS · 20 MIN

Six simple calculations that answer real questions

You do not need advanced mathematics to start. Learn to define a number, check its unit and interpret what it says.

1. Totalsum of all values

₦2,000 + ₦3,000 + ₦1,000 = ₦6,000 transport spent.

2. Average (mean)total ÷ number of values

₦6,000 ÷ 3 days = ₦2,000 per day. An unusually costly day can pull the mean upward.

3. Percentagepart ÷ whole × 100

₦3,000 of ₦6,000 = 50% of your three-day transport spending.

4. Differencenew value − old value

A ₦4,200 purchase versus ₦4,000 is ₦200 more.

5. Percentage change(new − old) ÷ old × 100

₦4,200 versus ₦4,000 is a 5% increase. If the old value is zero, this formula is undefined.

6. Profitrevenue − variable costs − fixed costs

₦36,000 sales − ₦21,600 ingredients − ₦6,000 overhead = ₦8,400 profit.

Always distinguish revenue from profit. Revenue is what customers paid for sales; profit is what remains after the relevant costs. A business may have growing revenue and falling profit.

Try it (5 minutes)

You budgeted ₦15,000 for data but spent ₦18,000. Calculate the difference (₦3,000) and percentage increase relative to the budget (20%). Explain whether your answer describes spending or profit.

WORKED CASE STUDY · 30–40 MIN

A small lunch kiosk: from receipts to a decision

Scenario: Amina runs a fictional lunch kiosk. Each meal sells for ₦1,500, costs ₦900 in ingredients and packaging, and her daily fixed costs are ₦6,000. She recorded meals sold over five weekdays. She asks: Which days earn the most profit, and how many meals must I sell to cover my costs?

Step 1: The raw observations and calculated results
DayMeals soldWeather*RevenueVariable costFixed costProfit
Monday24Dry₦36,000₦21,600₦6,000₦8,400
Tuesday18Rainy₦27,000₦16,200₦6,000₦4,800
Wednesday30Dry₦45,000₦27,000₦6,000₦12,000
Thursday20Rainy₦30,000₦18,000₦6,000₦6,000
Friday32Dry₦48,000₦28,800₦6,000₦13,200
Total124—₦186,000₦111,600₦30,000₦44,400

*Weather is an observation in this fictional example, not evidence that weather caused the sales differences.

Step 2: Calculate one day yourself

  1. Monday revenue = 24 meals × ₦1,500 = ₦36,000.
  2. Monday variable cost = 24 × ₦900 = ₦21,600.
  3. Monday profit = ₦36,000 − ₦21,600 − ₦6,000 = ₦8,400.
  4. Repeat for the other days. Sum each column and confirm the totals above.

Step 3: Interpret the whole week

Meals sold124
Weekly revenue₦186,000
Weekly profit₦44,400
Profit margin23.9%

Profit margin = ₦44,400 ÷ ₦186,000 × 100 ≈ 23.9%. Average daily profit = ₦44,400 ÷ 5 = ₦8,880. Friday had the highest recorded profit (₦13,200). Tuesday was lowest (₦4,800).

Step 4: Answer the break-even question

Contribution per meal

₦1,500 selling price − ₦900 variable cost = ₦600 per meal toward fixed costs and profit. Daily break-even quantity = ₦6,000 ÷ ₦600 = 10 meals. At exactly 10 meals, profit is ₦0 under these simplified assumptions.

Step 5: Test a “what if?”

Change the meals sold, selling price or cost below to see how one day’s results change. This is a simple scenario calculator—not a prediction of customer demand. Enter realistic, non-negative values.

At 24 meals: revenue ₦36,000 · total cost ₦27,600 · profit ₦8,400 · break-even 10 meals.

Step 6: Communicate a careful recommendation

A defensible conclusion

“In this five-day example, Friday produced the highest profit and Tuesday the lowest. We could test preparing more meals for Friday while tracking leftovers, customer demand and costs for several additional weeks.” We cannot conclude that rain caused lower sales from two rainy days, or that Friday will always be the strongest day.

Recreate it in Excel

Open the CSV in Excel. Its columns A–G contain date, day, units_sold, price_per_meal, unit_cost_per_meal, daily_fixed_cost and weather. Add three headers in H1–J1: revenue, variable_cost, profit.

H2: =C2*D2
I2: =C2*E2
J2: =H2-I2-F2

Fill H2:J2 down to row 6.
Weekly profit: =SUM(J2:J6)
Average daily profit: =AVERAGE(J2:J6)

Make a column chart with day on the horizontal axis and profit on the vertical axis. Include a descriptive title and label the currency. Check the chart values against the table before presenting it.

Your independent practice (15 minutes)
  1. Without checking the totals, calculate Wednesday’s profit from the downloaded CSV.
  2. Calculate the week’s total revenue and profit margin.
  3. Change Monday’s meals sold to 8 in the calculator: is the result a profit or a loss?
  4. Write one recommendation and one limitation in plain English.
Show worked answers

Wednesday: 30 × (₦1,500 − ₦900) − ₦6,000 = ₦12,000 profit. Weekly revenue = ₦186,000; profit margin ≈ 23.9%. Eight meals produce 8 × ₦600 − ₦6,000 = ₦1,200 loss. A reasonable next action is to test Friday stocking across more weeks; five days alone cannot establish a dependable seasonal pattern.

Your first mini-project: a seven-day spending diary

  1. Question:

    Which category uses the largest share of my weekly budget?

  2. Collect:

    Make a private sheet with date, category, amount and optional note. Record seven days; exclude identifying details you do not need.

  3. Check:

    Make sure every amount is in the same currency and avoid recording a purchase twice.

  4. Analyse:

    Sum by category, calculate each category’s percentage of the whole, and create one labelled chart.

  5. Explain:

    Write three sentences: what you found, one possible action, and what one week of data cannot tell you.

When are you ready for the main courses?

When you can explain what a row represents, check a total, distinguish revenue from profit, and state one limitation, you are ready to build on these skills. The first recommended technical course is Excel; SQL and Power BI follow naturally. You can revisit this guide any time.
